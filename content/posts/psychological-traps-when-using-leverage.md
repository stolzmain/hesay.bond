## The trap of imagined financial power

The first and most common psychological trap is the illusion of possessing superpowers. As soon as a trader opens a position with 1:20 or 1:100 leverage, they see impressive profit figures on the screen from even the slightest price movement. This triggers a powerful dopamine rush. The brain begins to perceive these virtual numbers as already earned capital. The trader stops seeing probabilities in the charts and starts seeing them as tools to fulfill their desires: buying a car, clearing debts, or changing their lifestyle. At this moment, the focus shifts from the analysis process to expecting the result. Professionalism gives way to daydreams, and any market correction against the position is perceived not as a signal to exit, but as a personal insult that the market is obligated to fix.

## Emotional paralysis during high volatility

High leverage dramatically narrows the comfort zone. While a 1-2 percent drawdown might seem like a standard part of the job when trading with your own funds, the same asset volatility can mean a total loss of the deposit when using 1:50 leverage. In such a situation, the fight-or-flight mechanism kicks in. The trader freezes or, conversely, begins to take chaotic actions. Instead of closing a losing trade at a stop-loss, the individual freezes in hope of a miracle. This paralysis of will is caused by the cost of the error becoming too high. The brain blocks rational thinking because admitting a loss with massive leverage is equivalent to admitting financial ruin. As a result, the trader simply watches as their account gets wiped out.

## The dangerous spiral of revenge trading

When a series of leveraged trades leads to significant losses, the revenge effect kicks in. Psychologically, it is extremely difficult for a trader to accept a 50 percent drop in their deposit in a single day. An overwhelming desire arises to get it back here and now. To achieve this, the trader increases the leverage even further, trying to cover all previous failures with a single trade. This is the classic tilt trap. The expected value works against the player at this moment, but the thrill of the gamble blinds them. In this case, leverage acts as a catalyst for self-destruction: it provides the technical capability to commit a fatal error, turning conscious trading into a high-speed casino. Each new increase in leverage only brings the moment of final liquidation closer.

## Distorted perception of success probability

There is a phenomenon where random success on a first high-leverage trade becomes a curse for a beginner. After receiving super-profits due to a fortunate set of circumstances, the trader reinforces a false neural connection in their subconscious: high leverage equals quick wealth. This leads to the devaluation of trading strategy and risk management. The trader starts to neglect analysis, relying instead on intuition and the power of leverage. They forget that leverage does not increase the probability of success; it only scales the result.
