## Biological traps of our consciousness

Psychology is the foundation upon which even the most mathematically sound strategies collapse. The human brain is not evolutionarily adapted for trading. Our survival instincts, such as fear and greed, work against us in market conditions. When the price moves against a position, false hope kicks in—a destructive feeling that forces traders to hold losing positions, waiting for a reversal. Conversely, when a position is profitable, the fear of losing that gain leads to premature exits, resulting in missed profits. As a result, the expected value becomes negative: the average loss significantly exceeds the average profit. A trader who cannot control their emotional impulses is doomed to systematic errors that no technical indicator or state-of-the-art software can fix.

## The paradox of discipline and gambling

Most beginners perceive trading as a game, which is a fatal misconception. This inevitably leads to overtrading. The desire to win back losses after a losing streak, known as tilt, forces traders to ignore their trading plan and enter the market where there are no objective conditions. The market is a cold environment that does not tolerate a personal approach. Trading requires boring, monotonous discipline comparable to a factory assembly line job. However, human nature craves excitement and validation of being right. Trying to prove your point to the market is the fastest route to account liquidation. A successful trader is not one who can guess price direction, but one who can execute their algorithm without deviation, even if the last five trades were closed at a stop-loss.

## Ignoring capital management rules

Even with a strategic edge, traders lose money due to a lack of risk management. Using excessive leverage is the fastest way to wipe out a trading account. Survivorship bias leads beginners to look at the random successes of aggressive traders on social media and believe that triple-digit monthly profits are the norm. In practice, trading without a stop-loss or averaging down on losing positions turns trading into roulette. Professionals risk no more than 1–2% of their capital per trade, while amateurs put 20%, 50%, or their entire deposit on the line at once. A bit of market noise or a sudden news flash takes these players out for good. A lack of understanding that a loss is a natural part of a business process, not a personal defeat, makes it impossible to build a sustainable long-term model.
