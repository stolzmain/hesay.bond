Unlike standard trading volume, which merely records the fact that a trade took place during a specific period, open interest demonstrates the real inflow or outflow of capital into an asset. Professional traders use this tool to determine the strength of a current move. If trading volume increases while open interest remains unchanged, it indicates that the market is experiencing only a redistribution of positions among existing participants rather than an influx of fresh capital. Understanding OI allows traders to distinguish short-term speculative noise from sustained institutional accumulation or distribution.

## Interrelation of Price Action and Volume

Analysis of marginal positions only makes sense when combined with price dynamics. There are four classic interpretation scenarios. Price growth alongside an increase in OI signals a strong bullish trend: new buyers are aggressively entering the market, fueling momentum. If the price rises but OI falls, it is a sign of a short squeeze—the closing of short positions amid losses, which points to trend weakness and an imminent reversal. Conversely, a price drop accompanied by an increase in OI confirms bearish sentiment, as new sellers exert downward pressure on the market. Finally, a decline in both price and open interest suggests long capitulation, which often precedes a consolidation phase. These patterns help avoid entering positions late in a move when the fuel for further growth or decline has already been exhausted.

## Psychology via Long/Short Ratio

The Long/Short Ratio is a powerful indicator of market sentiment. However, experienced analysts treat it as a contrarian indicator. When the ratio hits extreme values—for instance, when more than 70% of participants are in long positions—the probability of a sharp price drop increases. The market always seeks liquidity, which is concentrated behind the stop-loss levels of the majority. The more the crowd believes in a single scenario, the higher the risk of a liquidation cascade, which market makers use to fill their own orders. It is important to distinguish between retail positions and institutional players: often, small accounts are positioned long while smart money hedges risks or builds short positions through more complex instruments.

## Impact of Funding on Trend Sustainability

The Funding Rate is inextricably linked to marginal positions in perpetual futures. It ensures the contract price remains tethered to the spot market. A positive funding rate means long holders pay short holders to maintain their positions, which is characteristic of an overheated bull market. High holding costs for longs make them vulnerable: if the price stops rising, traders begin to close trades to avoid fees, thereby triggering a correction. A negative funding rate indicates bearish dominance. From an analytical perspective, extreme funding rates combined with anomalous OI are harbingers of whipsaws—sharp movements in both directions designed to clear the market of excess leverage.

## Liquidations as a Trigger for Momentum

Data on forced position liquidations is key to understanding market dynamics. When a large volume of marginal positions is liquidated, it creates a snowball effect. For example, the liquidation of a long position turns into a market sell order, which pushes the price lower, triggering the stop-losses of other participants. On a chart, this appears as a long candle wick.
