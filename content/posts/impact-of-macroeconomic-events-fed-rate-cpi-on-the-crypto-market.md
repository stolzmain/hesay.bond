## The Fed Rate as the Primary Trigger

The U.S. Federal Reserve interest rate is, in essence, the cost of money in the global economy. When the Fed raises rates to combat inflation, borrowing becomes more expensive, and the yield on "safe" assets, such as U.S. Treasury bonds, increases. In such periods, investors pull capital out of risk-on assets—stocks and cryptocurrencies—and rotate into cash or bonds. Conversely, lowering rates creates excess liquidity. Cheap dollars seek high yields, which pushes Bitcoin to new all-time highs. For the crypto market, a rate decision is not just a figure; it is a signal for the beginning or the end of a "cheap money" period, which is the primary fuel for bull rallies.

## Inflation and Bitcoin's Reaction

The Consumer Price Index (CPI) is the key measure of inflation and the main guide for the Fed. In theory, Bitcoin was conceived as a hedge against the debasement of fiat currencies. In practice, however, high inflation often causes a crypto market drop. This happens because the market perceives a high CPI as a harbinger of monetary tightening. If inflation exceeds forecasts, traders price in more aggressive rate hikes, leading to immediate sell-offs. It is crucial to understand: the crypto market reacts not so much to inflation itself, but to the expected regulatory response. Paradoxically, "bad" news for the economy often becomes "good" news for prices if it hints at an upcoming pivot in Fed policy.

## Correlation with the Stock Market

Recent statistics demonstrate a persistent correlation between cryptocurrencies and the Nasdaq 100 and S&P 500 indices. Since institutions view Bitcoin as a high-beta asset, it moves in lockstep with the tech sector, but with much higher amplitude. When macroeconomic data is released, we often see identical charts: a sharp impulse in the stock market is accompanied by an even more powerful move in crypto. This makes macro analysis an essential element of any trading strategy. Ignoring the state of the U.S. economy while trading digital assets is impossible today—it is equivalent to trying to predict the weather without looking at the sky.

## Liquidity as Growth Fuel

Beyond rates and inflation, the volume of the money supply (M2) is of critical importance. When global central banks print money, a portion of this liquidity inevitably settles in decentralized finance. The global liquidity cycle often precedes cryptocurrency price movements. Professional analysts track the Fed's balance sheet: its contraction (QT) usually coincides with bearish trends, while its expansion (QE) triggers parabolic growth. In this context, Bitcoin acts as a "sponge" absorbing excess money supply. This is precisely why macroeconomic events affecting the inflow or outflow of dollars from the system are of paramount importance for long-term price discovery.
