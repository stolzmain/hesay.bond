## Horizontal Levels: Zones of Interest

Horizontal levels are the most reliable elements of technical analysis because they are static and psychologically grounded. A support level is an area where buyer activity outweighs sell pressure, halting a price decline. Resistance, conversely, represents a price barrier where supply outweighs demand. It is important to understand that a level is not a thin line, but a price range or zone. The market rarely turns on a dime; more often, we see brief deviations beyond the level boundary, which inexperienced traders mistake for a breakout. Particular attention should be paid to flip levels—situations where former resistance becomes support after a breakout. This confirms a shift in market roles and serves as a strong signal for position entry.

## Price Dynamics and Trendlines

Trendlines help visualize the direction of the dominant market force. To construct an uptrend line, you need at least two consecutively higher lows, and for a downtrend, two lower highs. However, a line gains true strength only upon the third touch, which serves as confirmation that market participants recognize this diagonal level. The angle of the trendline also matters: an overly steep climb is often unsustainable and leads to a sharp correction, while a moderate slope indicates a healthy, prolonged trend. Professionals use trendlines not only to find entry points for bounces but also to spot signals of a potential market structure break upon a genuine breakout.

## Criteria for Valid Chart Constructions

Not all lines on a chart hold equal value. The strength of a level or trendline depends directly on two factors: timeframe and the number of touches. The higher the timeframe (e.g., daily or weekly charts), the more significant the level. Local lines on five-minute charts are often broken without a substantial reaction, creating false signals. The second important aspect is the freshness of the level. A level that has been tested repeatedly over a short period gradually thins out, as limit orders in that zone are filled, leaving no one to defend it. Ultimately, this leads to an impulsive breakout. You should also watch the volume: a true breakout or bounce from an important zone is always accompanied by a surge in trading activity.

## Practical Nuances of Charting

One of the main mistakes beginners make is overcomplicating the chart, often called spaghetti syndrome. When there are dozens of lines on the screen, the trader becomes paralyzed by an abundance of conflicting signals. A professional approach involves clearing the chart of unnecessary clutter. It is recommended to start the analysis from a higher timeframe, marking major historical levels, and then gradually move down to your working interval.
