According to the theory, any trend consists of five impulsive waves in the direction of the main trend and three corrective waves, labeled A, B, and C. The ABC corrective phase is of particular interest to traders, as it helps identify the end of a temporary pullback and prepare for a new impulse or a full trend reversal. ## Structural Features of the ABC Correction

The ABC corrective model is a three-wave structure that emerges after the completion of a five-wave bullish or bearish cycle. Its primary purpose is to let off steam and rebalance the market after a strong directional move. Traders often mistake a correction for the beginning of a new counter-trend, leading to premature entries. The ABC model can take various forms: zigzags, flats, or triangles. Each of these shapes has its own specific alternation rules and internal sub-waves. It is important to understand that a correction is always more complex than an impulse: it is filled with fakeouts, noise, and uncertainty. An analyst must be able to identify the completion of the fifth wave to switch in time to searching for an ABC pattern, rather than trying to trade against the momentum of a fading trend.

## Wave A as the Final Impulse

Wave A is the first stage of a correction. It is often mistaken for a standard pullback within an ongoing impulse. However, a professional eye notes a change in dynamics: trading volume may begin to drop or, conversely, spike sharply as major players take profit. Structurally, wave A can consist of either five sub-waves (in zigzags) or three (in flats). The main characteristic of this phase is psychological shock. Most traders still believe in the continuation of the old trend and use the decline as an opportunity to buy the dip. It is precisely this belief that forms the foundation for the next phase—wave B—which often becomes a trap for inexperienced market participants.

## Wave B and the Optimism Trap

Wave B is a reactive move against wave A, representing the market's attempt to return to previous highs (or lows in a downtrend). This is a phase of false optimism. During this stage, classic technical analysis patterns such as double tops or head and shoulders are formed. It is important to remember that wave B should never exceed the extreme of the completed fifth impulse wave in a classic zigzag. If the price approaches the peak but fails to break through, it is a powerful signal of bull weakness. From a wave analysis perspective, wave B is the optimal place to open positions in the direction of the future wave C, as the risk here is minimal and the profit potential is significant.

## Wave C as the Final Stage

Wave C is considered the most destructive for those holding positions against the trend. It always possesses a five-wave internal structure and moves in the same direction as wave A. In terms of strength and duration, it is often comparable to the first corrective wave, and sometimes significantly exceeds it. It is during wave C that the final disillusionment of the crowd occurs: those who bought the false bottom in wave A or during the B pullback are forced to close positions via stop-losses or margin calls.
